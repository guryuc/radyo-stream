Forgotten Elegance

Woman and Love

November 1, 2026 Ferzen Soydemir

This city, which I believe I have lost, was once the center of happiness, of manners, of nobility and of courtesy. Cities have their mysteries, their unhistorical formations, their wars, their conquests; they also have their customs, their traditions, their beauties and their insatiabilities... Living these to the full in the past was my happiness. Every city has a story, and thinking about it makes me feel good. Now I want to speak of the city of times that slipped through my fingers, that I could not hold onto. The old dignity, the reputation, the understanding, the loves and the embraces are no longer to be found in the people of this city, the pearl of the world, with its unique beauties.

The right to object is reserved, sir. This city has disappeared a little more each day before my eyes. Of course, it did not evaporate, it did not fly away, it did not collapse, it did not melt. But it went, it was lost somehow. Are you aware? We can no longer talk. Even when we speak, the words are in rebellion. We are narrowing our vocabulary, as it were. What is it to speak with three or five words? When we cannot convey what we mean... Yes, this city has received a lot of migration, it has changed a lot. I cannot bring myself to say it, but it has become provincial. The language has changed, the words have, the understandings too. This bothers me deeply.

Let them come. Let them come, but whatever region they come from, let them not bring their customs. As they say: the aim should be not to live in Istanbul, but to keep Istanbul alive. When we say "Let them adapt to my city, let them learn my customs," let them not impose on me the necessity to adapt to them. This, I think, is the very essence of disappearance. Many years ago, there was an 8-15 ferry departing from Kadıköy that became the subject of songs. The inside of that ferry was quality itself. It was a ferry used by everyone going to work at that hour in the morning. People would sit in the places they had determined for themselves. Respect was so complete that we would not dare sit in anyone's place. I am speaking of a simple and silent rule of respect.

What else have we not lost in this city in similar fashion? Greetings, smiles, measured behaviors, courtesies... These were the symbols of social life. No doubt you live and keep these alive. I am speaking of the general situation. I am sure you are as complaining as I am. It is a dream, of course... I wish cities were like countries. Time has instilled this thought in me. If the people of every city were happy by living together, it would be, to my mind, very pleasant. I too would embrace my city more. Do you think I am thinking too utopically?

When a person thinks of themselves, they become a bit self-centered. As a result, I now live in a lost city that no one searches for anymore. I expect value, respect and understanding from the people in this city that has become ordinary despite its unique stories. I wait and I wait... Let them give me back this city where I was born. With its sea, its shores, its houses... With its customs and traditions, its elegant women and gentlemanly men, its children and their innocence... I will not say "what is past is past"; I will try to reflect, teach and keep alive the beauties.

They say to me, "Your fashion has passed." Do you think the fashion of good and beautiful things ever passes?

Postcard

Memorable Experiences

November 1, 2026 Nilüfer Gürler

Her plane had landed at JFK. When she looked up at the sky through the tiny window, it was hard to tell from which direction the day, still hidden behind the morning darkness, was blowing. As she said to herself, "I should check the weather," her gaze drifted to the careful, rhythmic movements of the ground attendant. With her tiny frame beneath the nose of the giant aircraft and her two bright red magic wands, the woman was guiding the huge plane that had just been roaring at 30,000 feet into its parking spot, docile as a lamb...

Inside the great bird that had shaken the dust of the long journey from its wings, the energy was at its peak! The 380 passengers, who had been cut off from what was happening outside for ten hours, were clinging to their phones like hungry babies to their mother's breast. A colorful mosaic of languages, a humming cloud of conversation had filled the interior of the plane. Among all these languages, her ears had caught a word or two of Turkish. In the city she lived in, at least three languages were spoken at any moment. Although Turkish was not one of them, her beautiful mother tongue would always rise to the surface like a drop of olive oil falling into water, wherever she was.

Yes; a male voice was explaining that he was filling out the customs forms and yes, that he had his passport in his hand, yes yes, that no matter how long the visa line was, his uncle would wait, of course, inshallah, of course he would call them back.

Suddenly she remembered her own first landing at JFK. That day, when she first looked out of the plane window, New York had a secret she did not yet know and had to keep tightly hidden. She had come to stay, not to visit. It would happen somehow, this much had already happened, and for now that was enough.

A great adventure that began with the ignorant courage of a youth left far behind... "Dad, is Brooklyn with one 'o' or two?" "Okay, I've filled out my form and put it between the visa pages..." She understood the excitement of the man she was overhearing very well.

She was a university student in Istanbul. Her father's financial situation had only covered the first year. When she realized she had to work to continue her education, one of her uncles, all of whom were engineers, found her a job alongside two of his friends, also engineers. The office was in Taksim, within walking distance of the girls' dormitory where she stayed in Nişantaşı.

She had two bosses at the office: two unpleasant men with receding hair, pot bellies, exhibiting all the characteristics of their forties.

The tastelessly furnished office needed to be opened, the phone answered, visitors received, notes taken, and the occasional letters typed. The black telephone on the desk was an old model, almost antique, with a receiver heavy enough to be used as a weapon in self-defense. The typewriter, which she barely knew how to use, was fortunately electric and a fairly new model.

After a while, she wanted to brighten up the little corner of her desk a bit. Hanging something on the wall would be good. With the mind she had at the time, she decided a map would be nice. "And I'll look at it and dream of traveling," she thought.

She had a huge world map in her hands that connected all the major airports where Pan Am flew in an arc shape with blue lines. She hung it nicely on the wall behind her desk.

Despite the disapproving looks of her first boss, whom she described at the time as "the old man" but who was actually only 45, she managed to keep her map. "What's the connection? Is this a travel agency?" her boss had grumbled.

One morning she was caught. While she was drawing the line between New York and Istanbul with a thick red marker and dreaming, her boss had stood behind her. "Ooo, so Istanbul-New York is red now? Vaaay, is our girl going to New York or something?" he had asked in a mocking voice. She immediately hid the pen behind her back and turned toward him. With a shy voice, saying "No, not yet," she wanted to disappear behind her desk. "One day, maybe," she said, turning back to the typewriter. For now, it was just one of the messages she had sent to the universe, that was all.

The man laughed with a somewhat unnecessary exaggeration. "I think you should improve your typing skills first to earn the money for your English lessons," he had walked toward his desk with a mocking air.

She had been very angry. When she thickened the line on the map a little more with the red marker before leaving the office that day, she had watched with pleasure as the paint went through the paper and stained the snow-white wall behind it, and her anger had eased a little.

Two years had passed since this heartbreak. She still hadn't learned English but had mastered the typewriter quite well. She had finished school and no longer had a reason to work. Very shortly afterward, by chance, her dreams came true. Her aunt in Brooklyn had invited her. Everything had gone well, she had gotten her visa. She was going!

She had set out, leaving everything behind, including the red stain on the wall. She had wanted to fly with Pan Am, but since her mother and father would not agree to send her alone and buying three Pan Am tickets would be too expensive, the three of them had flown together from Istanbul to JFK on one of Yugoslav Airlines' discounted flights. The flood of passengers inside the plane, with their hand luggage, their dogs and their children, had begun their parade toward the open door. She decided to wait, she was in no hurry anyway. The Turkish man passing by her was still very tense on the phone. She sent him a smile as he passed in front of her, but the man's excitement-filled eyes had passed without looking at her. Her smile hung in the air. When it was her turn to leave, she thanked the cabin crew, whose every state showed they had hit rock bottom in their energy reserves, and left the plane. Rolling her small hand suitcase and camera bag, she passed through the sleepy passengers waiting to board the plane that the arriving passengers had just vacated, and headed toward the passport control point.

45 years had passed since the day she first landed. Because of flights whose number she had forgotten, she had more or less experienced the changes the airport had undergone. The only thing that had not changed was the chaos in the environment. The number of shops without doors or walls, with their brightly lit signs that made the place echo, spilling their colorful goods into the corridors, had increased many times over. There was no need for her to understand the language the couple walking ahead of her was speaking. The woman was clearly caught in the spell of shopping and was not even paying attention to the man beside her tugging at her arm toward the exit. She couldn't help but laugh. They too had dragged her mother out like this when they got off the JAT. Her late mother had loved it, she would touch everything, whether she bought it or not. Her father had no tolerance at all for shopping talk.

In the passport control building, her turn had already come in the queue of citizens advancing quickly. As the sullen customs officer stamped her passport and said welcome home, her eyes, scanning the crowded "non-citizens" queue one last time, could not find the Turkish man who had taken her back to her youth at the speed of light. Half asleep, with thoughts still keeping alive the remnants of past memories in her head, she had jumped on the air train between Jamaica and JFK. The day was slowly dawning. When a baby screamed from somewhere near the back of the train, the train had arrived at her stop. With a familiar agility, she slipped down the stairs to the lower floor. Outside the station, she raised her hand to hail a yellow taxi that would take her to the city. Shortly afterward, she was making her way through the flood of sounds, buildings, traffic and people. 45 minutes later, when the taxi headed into the tunnel passing under Park Avenue, the memories she had paused in her mind had started moving again. One day, for a reason she could not fully remember, she had found herself on Park Avenue. When she looked up and saw 200 Park Avenue rising with its 59 floors above Grand Central Station, she had stood rooted to the ground. Of course, there was no shortage of buildings in Manhattan, so what was special about this one? This building was the Pan Am Building! At the very top, in giant blue letters, it was almost calling out from the top of the city toward the south. Exactly three years had passed since that unpleasant memory in Taksim. Now she was looking at the building of the company that adorned her dreams.

Forgetting everything else, she had entered the lobby of the building with the bearing of a victorious athlete walking on the podium to receive a medal. She did not yet have a camera. To keep this magnificent lobby alive in her memories, she had decided to buy a postcard and slip it into her diary. After idling a little, she had become uncomfortable with the looks of the security guard at the door, and fearing that he might ask her something and she would not be able to answer since her English was not yet good, she had left the way she came, without drawing too much attention.

The old Pan Am building she was now passing under belonged to MetLife. It had its name on top. Who had moved into that little office on La Martin Street, she wondered? What had happened to the red stain on the white wall? It must have been painted over long ago, she thought. That wall had been her confidant, the one with whom she shared her grand dreams silently, without ever mocking them. So be it, the building had changed hands, but the lived experiences had been inherited. Neither paints nor real estate agents could destroy the traces of those exact moments left in the past. As she closed the taxi door and released her sleepy body into the dense noise of the city, she noted in her mind: "I must find that postcard."

Istanbul's Different Identities...

Another Istanbul

November 1, 2026 Ahmet Sinan Sarıkaya

Finishing a workday and to continue my photography project, I set off from Sanayi District by metro. While thinking on one hand "What will come my way?", on the other hand, with the planning of how to create and shoot the images I had conceived in my mind within the streets, I had arrived at the stop I was going to get off at. Getting off the vehicle, I started walking from the Golden Horn Bridge toward Küçükpazar.

There were lots of people around, tourists visiting Istanbul from different geographies, different languages and human types. With a curious observation, I was trying to eavesdrop on what was being said. I was pricking up my ears to every voice with "Will it give me a photo story?" while on the other hand, I was in a back-and-forth in my head like "I wonder if I could catch a better moment further ahead?"

While experiencing all this, the human voices in the street began to sound like music joining in universal communication. The voices were sometimes reggae, sometimes jazz, or a reflection of ethnic music belonging to their traditions. Suddenly, the words of Nina Simone, who said at a university of eighteen thousand people, "I won't sing my songs just for the three hundred black friends here," came to my mind. Indeed, jazz first started as ethnic, then became universal.

As I said, I continued on my way with the dream of "Could I catch a better moment further ahead?" I started walking the streets; while wandering with the thought of how and what to photograph, the songs of Muddy Waters, Joan Baez, Buena Vista Social Club, Goran Bregovic and many more were echoing in my head. In the end, I found myself in front of Ahmet Abi's coffee house. I ordered a tea and started chatting with familiar faces. While the conversations and greetings continued, I noticed someone I had never seen before to my left. I asked, nobody knew him.

With the stance you see in the photo, the way he answered his ringing phone, and the felt jacket he was wearing, he was a different person. His tone of voice was very melodic, his style different from many of the people there. I greeted him by making eye contact. He began with "Hello, my friend"; the conversation deepened. At that very moment, he began to talk about his involvement with music. It was Fırat Başkale, by his known stage name. With the music he made, I understood that he established a connection with the world through the language of the geography he was born in.

We talked about concerts, he told me about the music he made ethnically. He shared his views with me on this subject, explaining how he tried to interpret traditional music in Western norms, how he could transform it into something universal as in ethnic jazz form. He talked about his "Orient rock" experiments. It's easy to say, but a full 50 years of musical history lay behind him.

The steps I took with the thought "Could I catch a better moment further ahead?" had led me to an artist. Let Victor Hugo's words complete this evening: "Music expresses that which cannot be said and on which it is impossible to be silent."*

Thank you very much, Fırat Başkale...

*The source of this famous saying by Victor Hugo, whose original French is "Ce qu'on ne peut dire et ce qu'on ne peut taire, la musique l'exprime.": Book: William Shakespeare (1864), Section: Second Book (Livre Deuxième - L'Art et la Science / Art and Science), Chapter I. Page: Although the page number varies according to the edition and translation, it appears on page 80 in the original 1864 French first edition (A. Lacroix, Verboeckhoven et Cie). (Note: Victor Hugo wrote this book in memory of the 300th anniversary of William Shakespeare's death; he shared his thoughts on literature, art, aesthetics and music in this work.)

Hidden in the Mirror

Two Cities One Woman

November 1, 2026 Fatma Altun

As the first light of the day seeped through the trees, in the damp, moss-scented silence of the forest, only her timid footsteps echoed. On her walk to clear her mind as she did every morning, her feet had again dragged her to the familiar path. She sat on a rock and gently slipped into the small notebook she took from her pocket the tale the nature whispered to her. Since her childhood, she had invented tales, consoled herself. When she came here, she had decided; even if she didn't remember many of them, she would write those stories that were the legacy of her childhood. When the words ran out, the notebook went back into the pocket, the pen fell silent. Feeling the voices inside her calm down, she headed back home.

When she stepped into the courtyard, it was not the usual absolute solitude that greeted her, but a pile of supplies unloaded from the town hardware store's veteran truck pulled up in front of the door. The hardware store owner had left the materials out in the open. Cement bags, wooden slats, renovation tools... In a geography where rain could fall at any moment, leaving such delicate goods out in the open was not wise. She silenced the voice cursing the hardware store owner by focusing her mind on the mess in front of her. Instead of carrying this load into the house, she thought of taking it to the two-story old structure rising at the other end of the garden. The lower floor of this structure, built on the steep slope of the land, had once been used as a barn; when viewed from the road level, it was at zero point. The hayloft on the upper floor, which her father had converted into a workshop for a period, could be entered level from the garden side. Carrying the goods there would be much easier.

Right next to the workshop was the woodshed, and behind it lay the family cemetery covered with damp leaves. All those areas needed to be cleaned, the weeds removed, but she had not yet touched them. The world of the dead could wait for now behind the renovation rush of the living. Still, out of respect for the ancestors lying there, she decided not to delay this work too long.

Pushing the creaking wooden door, she entered her father's old workshop. The faint light filtering in made visible the dust particles floating in the air, the thick cobwebs that had taken up residence in the corners of the ceiling. While looking around to make room for the new tools, she was surprised by the order of that old equipment where time had frozen. Rusty saws hanging on the wall, screwdrivers, files lined up on the workbench, boxes full of nails of all sizes... Every object had been placed with a meticulous precision. After the bright and artificial glass tables of the high buildings and plazas in the city, this dusty workshop was like the naked face of reality. Her father had not even attended high school; officially he was a primary school graduate, but each time he would say, "I studied a full eleven years, I count as a high school graduate." He would talk at every opportunity about the quality of the education they had received back then; now she understood much better what her father meant. Though her father had grown up a bit mischievous, with an overly free spirit, since he was orphaned at a young age, still with his immense practical intelligence and hand skills that worked wonders, he was often referred to as a genius around him. Somehow he understood every job. There were those who claimed he could even make a human in his workshop if he had enough materials. He collected everything, wouldn't throw away even a small piece. He generally turned out to be right; what everyone called trash was treated as spare parts in this workshop and always came in useful.

While thinking about these, with a smile settling on her face, she began emptying the shelves behind the workbench. Among the shelves, she found a small mirror wedged in the corner, its frame falling apart. She remembered this too. Sometimes when her father shaved in the courtyard, he would put the mirror in front of a stone; he would proudly tell that it had passed from his grandfather to his father, and from his father to him. Once she had been caught using it without permission, and he had told her that the mirror passed from father to son. When he saw the strength and rightness of her objection, he said, "It can be yours when I die." No one ever spoke of the mirror again so that her father would never die. So he had hidden it here.

When she wiped the thick layer of dust on it with her fingertips, she saw that distant woman looking back at her from the mirror's secret. She stopped smiling… Just as she was about to put the mirror aside, a small yellowed piece of paper folded and wedged into the crack behind the wooden frame caught her attention. She pulled the paper out as if with tweezers. She recognized her father's angular, simple but masterful handwriting. Only these lines were scribbled: "A person weaves a cage for themselves everywhere, and then looks for the door to get out of it."

This simple sentence, in the silence of the workshop, opened a completely different door in her mind. She grabbed the mirror and the paper and rushed home. She sat at the desk she had placed by the window closest to the constantly burning stove in the kitchen. She opened her computer and began to write: "Italian thinker Emanuele Coccia, in his work Metamorphoses, says that human beings do not have a single and innate fixed essence, that life is actually a continuous process of form-changing, of 'migrating from shell to shell', and thus leaves a map on this path. Just like the cocoon a caterpillar weaves while transforming into a butterfly, the forms we put on to survive are not artificial lies, but an effort to seek a new existence.

The years spent in the air-conditioned, high-ceilinged plazas of big cities teach a person most of all to put on those armors. The clothes chosen while standing in front of the wardrobes after waking up at the crack of dawn are actually the costumes of that scene. A woman who puts her rubber shoes, whose laces she has replaced with satin ribbons to feminize them, under a masculine suit of black silk blend, plays her own theatre while walking through male-dominated corridors. When she unbuttons her jacket as she is about to sit at her desk, she is amused to see how a tiny bit of décolletage that spills out paralyzes the unauthorized and crude gazes around. This balance she establishes between masculinity and flamboyance is her captivity behind invisible bars. When society locks the woman in that cage, the woman learns to exist in her own cell with these games. But when she gets too used to those shells, the forms eventually stick to her skin.

When a person escapes those neon lights and takes refuge in a ruined hut; that's when the cocoon cracks. When she looks at the small mirror she found on a dusty workbench, what remains is not the woman in the plaza, but a bald-headed, pitch-black-eyed little girl looking at her from beyond the mirror. The only thing a person is left with is their own naked existence under the shed shells." As the text flowed on the screen, she pressed the "Publish" button. With the familiar confirmation window that appeared, she took a deep breath and slowly lowered the lid of her laptop.

Nisyan, that is, forgetting; at the conceptual level represents the flawed relationship of a human with time and its own mind. In the silence of the countryside that suddenly filled the room, her gaze drifted to the small mirror on the table and her father's old note. When she took the piece of paper in her hand again and turned it over, she realized that it was not an ordinary paper, that it had been torn not from a notebook, but from a much older book. The faint remnants of mysterious lines written in old letters at the edge of the paper could have belonged to one of the books in her grandfather's room that her father had entered without permission in his childhood. The unique library of her great-grandfather, full of books that were always talked about but of which not even a single witness remained, had never been found. When she asked her father, he had said, "My grandfather hid the books he guarded like his eyes when I last cleared out his room. Even I couldn't find them. I think he buried them." The manuscript was perhaps a piece of a page torn without knowing its value by mischievous childish hands from a precious book. This small paper that froze time was the first whisper that opened the door to a great mystery that everyone would pursue.

While Touring Adana and Its Surroundings

Through Begüm's Eyes and Words

November 1, 2026 Begüm Özüer

We toured Tarsus historically, saw it, now let's get into the mood of eating and drinking a little. Our country is an incredible geography in terms of food; in general, when you look at European countries, things like French cuisine, Italian cuisine etc. are mentioned about their cuisines, but when you look at the detail, you generally see meat, pasta, cheese; yes, it may be interesting because of the way it is cooked, but come on, try cooking a karnıyarık, an analı-kızlı soup, a stuffed grape leaves with olive oil, don't you?

That's why Gaziantep being a gastronomy center, Adana's kebabs, the breakfast of Van and Diyarbakır, every kind of dish made with anchovy in the Black Sea, kuymak etc., the article goes on and on like this. Now, where shall we start eating in Tarsus and of course Mersin?

When we first went to Adana, to the questions of acquaintances about where to eat what, what to see, they said have breakfast in Mersin with şıralı börek, we got the address and went. Of course I was going to try something new, so I ordered şıralı, and my husband ordered cheese börek, but I didn't know what şıralı was; the böreks came, and my börek was a sweet börek with syrup as you know, and since we are not Italian, having breakfast with sweet börek in the morning didn't seem very appealing to me, so I also ordered my good old cheese börek. And I couldn't tell whether it was the happiness or the fiasco of trying something new.

Let's continue from Mersin; the famous ice cream maker Halil is known for his cezerye, although cezerye is now everywhere, but this place and Yeni Uğur in Adana are very good, and Yeni Uğur's tahini especially comes to Istanbul by cargo, and its price is a laughable amount for Istanbul. Yes, the ice cream maker Halil also has a kerebiç filling walnut or pistachio cookie, but what is interesting is the foam made with the Çöven root placed on top, but it didn't suit my taste.

After eating tantuni in Mersin, we can move on to Tarsus. Although tantuni geographically belongs to Mersin, it is now possible to find it everywhere, but of course the good ones. If you go to Tarsus hungry, you immediately eat a hot pastırma hummus made by hand at Akın Humus, along with about half a loaf of bread, it's such a magnificent thing.

Its feature is that it is generally made the same way in Tarsus, by pounding by hand. Then let's not eat the hummus with too much bread and enter the bazaar to eat a coaster-sized lahmacun; the reason it's called coaster-sized is that it's really the size of the bottom of a tea glass, and believe me, it doesn't matter whether you're hungry or full, you sit and eat it, I'm not writing the number.

Now, it would be good to eat some dessert; first of all, there is the well-known künefe in Tarsus and there is a famous künefe shop, but since it is too touristic, it seems to have turned into factory production, but when you ask where to eat it, that shop comes up; I leave the künefe subject to you.

While wandering around wondering what other dessert to eat, we started looking for the shop to eat the non-spilling kazandibi I saw on social media. And we found it, you really turn it upside down and it doesn't spill, but it's not the taste we know either, is it good? is it different? Yes.

Also as a dessert, there is the Karakuş Dessert, a dessert where plenty of walnuts are put inside the dough, fried and syruped; people take one while passing in front of the shop and continue, a crunchy, pleasant thing. Yes, we ate and drank, of course there are more varieties of food, but they are generally made at home on special days, I shared what I tasted here. I hope if your path falls to Mersin, Adana and Tarsus, I will have guided you even a little. Until we meet in the Black Sea in my next article…

The Tears of Rain on the Window: An Autumn Accounting

Those Looking in the Same Direction

November 1, 2026 Elif Biçer

Autumn, with each arrival, greets us with the unique colors of sorrow and serenity. The trees shed the tones of green and begin to turn yellow, brown and crimson. Then they are defeated by the wind, by the rain; they shed their leaves one by one to the earth.

Feeling the Rhythm of Nature Being on the roads in this season, collecting memories with photographs, and a person being alone with themselves by reading a book is a separate pleasure. Witnessing that rustle that comes out while walking calmly on the dried leaves makes us feel that we have reconnected with nature.

Sometimes a person becomes melancholic while walking and murmurs, "Just like these leaves, what stories have departed from our lives one by one..." At that very moment, turning the sorrow of the season into joy by tossing the fallen leaves with our hands, fixing these moments with a photograph frame, makes life delightful.

The Feast of Surrender in the Sky Is it possible to watch the stork migration, the unchanging rhythm of autumn, and not be amazed by the serenity of those farewells, by their flawless navigational ability? The storks, using the warm air currents rising over the sun-warmed earth, present a visual feast in the sky. Instead of constantly flapping their wings, they surrender themselves to these air currents and display the most beautiful example of staying in the flow and surrender.

As the coolness of the evening accompanies the autumn days, the colorful life of summer gives way to calmness. While coffees are sipped in front of windows, books are embraced more often. And the tears of rain on the window sometimes remind us of joyful, sometimes deep emotional memories.

The Balance Sheet Period of Life Actually, autumn is the accounting period of life; it is, as it were, the bookkeeping of the past months. These days we question how much we have achieved the goals we set. We put our pluses and minuses before us, and shape our new plans in this way.

Now is the time to focus on the values each falling leaf adds to our lives and to greet fresh springs with new energies. That our losses spread over the years are few; that our gains such as friendship, tolerance and love multiply is the greatest profit of our conscience accounting. Could there be a better balance sheet equation than this?

Football

The Story of Small Things (Essays on life's overlooked details)

November 1, 2026 Fulya Yılmaz

Do you remember that magical moment when you thought everything in your life was going perfectly, when you said, "Now I've won!"? That second when you felt you had hit the bullseye, caught that love you dreamed of, or made the biggest move of your career... And right after that, an invisible hand shoving all your joy down your throat?

If you have tasted this feeling, you have already met that most ironic, most merciless rule of life: Offside.

Today I want to announce to you not the whispers of great events, dazzling successes or victories that make headlines; but of an old football that faded away in a neighborhood, and perhaps the whispers of our own story hidden behind that ball.

As Sami Paşazade Sezai whispered in his "Little Things"; the real tragedy of life is hidden inside those small details that no one turns to look at. Just like that old leather football silently waiting in the mud puddle that rain water has opened at the foot of a garden wall.

Some of the hexagonal stitches on it had come undone, its color had turned gray with the soot of the street. Whereas just a few months earlier, it was the most precious treasure in the world for the children of the neighborhood. It was the sole ruler of those imaginary castles set up between two stones in the back streets. While the children ran after it, they forgot time, and the street would echo with laughter mixed with the smell of freshly baked bread from the bakery.

Then, one day...

One of the most merciless rules of life quietly slipped in among them.

That evening, Ali, the most talented and most impatient child of the neighborhood, made the most perfect run he could possibly make. Kicking up the dust of the street, he lunged forward to catch perhaps the most beautiful pass life had offered him. His heartbeats were keeping rhythm with a tremendous victory. Before the defense line even understood what was happening, he met the ball in a completely empty position. Ali touched the leather ball with the inside of his foot so elegantly, so hard... The ball slid into the top corner amid the goalkeeper's helpless gaze and became a goal by passing between the two stones.

Ali, with arms wide open, was doing a victory lap in the street, his friends were running toward him. It was the happiest, most peak moment of his twelve-year life. At that very second, life's ironic and hidden referee blew his whistle. The children of the opposing team, with their hands in the air, were shouting: "Offside! You had already crossed the line when the pass was made!"

Ali's tremendous enthusiasm, his blind love for the goal, had passed that fine line he needed to stop at by a millimeter. He was too eager, he had rushed too much. The goal he scored was perhaps the most beautiful goal in the world, but his "timing" was wrong. The position was cancelled.

The women sitting on the sidewalk by the road, munching their sunflower seeds on one hand and watching the children on the other, could not make sense of this noise and looked at each other and whispered: "What happened now? Didn't the child put the ball in the right place? Why didn't they count it?"

To that curse of "doing the right thing at the wrong time," which men have built theories on for a lifetime and considered the greatest tragedy of life, women were obviously looking from a completely different window. Perhaps in the eyes of women, life was too big and too worth living to be confined to these mechanical calculations men invented for themselves, to millimetric lines. For them, if the ball went into the goal, it went in; if effort was given, it was given. The hidden calculations behind, the split seconds in which the pass was made, and those invisible boundary lines were nothing but empty details that shadowed the joy of life. While women embraced the beauty of the moment and the effort itself and looked at the result; men drowned in the irony of that invisible line.

As required by the rule of the game, as the penalty for the offside, the ball was placed for the opposing team, right at the foot of the garden wall of the grumpy old man at the end of the street. An indirect free kick was to be taken. When the opposing team's child threw himself at the ball with fury, the leather sphere this time cleared the high walls of that unlucky garden and fell inside. The old man did not give the ball back again, he threw it into the darkest corner of the garden, into oblivion. And thus the most aesthetic goal in the world disappeared without being able to change the scoreboard.

As the ball stayed there, it began to fade. Just like people's enthusiasm that begins with great hopes and an early excitement, and fades away by crashing into the harsh timing walls of life. For a while the children looked behind that wall, at that unjust position taken from them, with longing eyes; then each one got caught up in the "VAR" reviews of their own life and scattered in other directions. Some became apprentices in their father's shop, some were scattered to other cities in pursuit of their bread.

Now let's be honest and lean back and think.

How many of us scored the most aesthetic goal in the world in our work life, in our relationships or in our dreams, and that goal was cancelled just because of "timing"? How many of us, just when we opened our hearts completely to someone we loved very much, were stopped by life's assistant referee with the gesture "You got too excited, turn back now"? How many of us had our efforts, given by burning the candle at both ends, left to fade in that grumpy old man's garden?

Offside is the way life teaches us not how much we want something, but how patiently we can wait on the right line. That fading leather ball may still be mourning somewhere your most beautiful but invalid goal.

Because perhaps life is not the game of those who boast only of hitting the top corner; but of those who run at the right time, knowing when the flag will be raised..

CHAPTER 6: THRESHOLD

When I walked out of the massive gate of the Grand Bazaar, the sky had taken on a leaden darkness. The rain, increasing its intensity, had covered the city like a curtain. While people were rushing in a panic to take shelter under the eaves, a man slipping through the crowd bumped hard into my shoulder.

I was shaken; as I held onto a wall to regain my balance, I looked at the man's silhouette rapidly moving away. At that moment, the silver ring on his right hand glinted: Ouroboros. The snake biting its own tail...

The Guardians were much closer than I had thought. For them, the snake was not just a symbol; it was both a protective and a destructive force, the ancient cycle of the passage from ignorance to wisdom, of the transformation of the impure into the pure—alchemy. The path to the secret knowledge passed through entering this cycle. From the depths of my mind, a dusty sentence surfaced: "Non omnis figura sigillum est. Sed omne sigillum figuram abscondit." (Not every shape is a seal. But every seal hides a shape.)

Suddenly I stopped. I had understood my biggest mistake: I kept trying to read the seal like a text. Whereas the seal was there not to be read, but to mask something. The letters were not messages, but veils. If Ouroboros points to four directions, the place I needed to look was not the letters themselves, but the geometric spaces they pointed to.

I quickly headed to the Beyazıt State Library. The silence inside was heavier, more uncanny than ever. Thinking of the collector's words, I pulled out from among the shelves that very old catalog that Agrippa had cited. After hours of scanning, I found a note whose binding was about to fall apart: Ex Bibliotheca Constantinopolitana. (From the Library of Constantinople.) Right below it was that short phrase that froze my blood: "Clavis non in litteris est." (The key is not in the letters.) And at the very bottom of the page, again those two letters: L.I.

While looking at the photograph on the table, I suddenly stopped. The letters 'L' and 'I'... In the Middle Ages, these letters could be not only sounds but also numbers. Latin L (50) and I (1)... In total, 51. This fresco in Florence was not the end of a word, but the 51st item of a list. If this was an inventory, the 51st item was that sacred relic with the lily symbol smuggled to Florence. But the inventory did not end here. I needed to reach the next number, 52. 52... The weeks of the year? Or the completion of a sacred cycle?

My breath tightened. The fresco in Florence was not a source, but the visible last link of this strange chain. The real "threshold" was here, in Istanbul. When I raised my head, I realized that the librarian's desk was empty. In the whole building, no one was left but me. The noise of the rain outside made the absolute silence inside even more crushing. In the catalog of the Beyazıt Library, the same record appeared before me three times: Nicetae Choniatae Historia. The same work, in three different copies... But in the third copy there was a note: "Folium LII deest." (The 52nd leaf is missing...)

Suddenly, I heard the huge main doors of the library close with a great noise. As the echo rippled through the corridors, the electricity went out. The darkness spread through the room like ink. In the silent witness of thousands of books, I was trapped inside a giant tomb.

I held the weak light of my phone in front of me. The sound of my footsteps echoed on the high ceiling of the library like the footsteps of a stranger. The exit was closed; or perhaps the real path was in the very heart of the darkness.
